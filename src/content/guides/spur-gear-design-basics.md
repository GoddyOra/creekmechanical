Open a gear catalog or a gear generator for the first time and you're immediately asked for three numbers — module, pressure angle, tooth count — with no explanation of what any of them actually do to the part. Pick badly and you'll either generate a gear that doesn't mesh with its mate, one that's needlessly oversized, or one whose teeth are too weak for the load. This guide covers what each parameter actually controls, why the standard values are what they are, and how to move through the design decisions in a sensible order.

## Module: the parameter that sets overall size

Module is the fundamental size parameter for a metric gear, defined simply as:

**Module (m) = pitch diameter / number of teeth**

It's easiest to think of module as "how much pitch diameter each tooth is worth." A module-2 gear with 20 teeth has a 40 mm pitch diameter; the same 20 teeth cut at module-3 gives a 60 mm pitch diameter — same tooth count, bigger gear, because each tooth is claiming more space. Module is expressed in millimeters and standardized to a preferred series (1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, and so on) so that standard cutting tools and hobs exist for it — picking a non-standard module usually means a custom (and expensive) tool, so unless you have a specific reason not to, stay on the standard series.

Imperial gearing uses the inverse concept, diametral pitch (teeth per inch of pitch diameter), which is why the two systems don't mix — a metric-module gear and an imperial-diametral-pitch gear will not mesh together correctly even if their pitch diameters happen to be close, because the tooth shape and spacing are governed by fundamentally different unit conventions.

## Pitch diameter is a reference, not a real feature

It's worth being explicit about this because it trips people up: the pitch diameter isn't a surface you can measure with calipers. It's the diameter of an imaginary circle — the pitch circle — where two meshing gears' teeth theoretically roll against each other without slipping, the same way two friction discs of the same diameters would. Every other gear dimension is defined relative to it: the tip of the tooth sits one module beyond the pitch circle (the addendum), and the root sits 1.25 modules inside it (the dedendum), for standard full-depth teeth. That asymmetry — more room cut below the pitch line than added above it — exists specifically to leave clearance so the tip of one gear's tooth doesn't bottom out in the root of its mate.

## Pressure angle: what it actually controls

Pressure angle defines the angle of the line of action — the direction along which force actually transmits between meshing teeth — relative to a line tangent to the pitch circles. It's usually 20° on modern gears; older designs sometimes used 14.5°, and you'll still encounter it on legacy equipment.

A larger pressure angle produces a wider, stronger tooth base (more material supporting the load) and allows a smaller minimum tooth count before the tooth profile undercuts itself during cutting. The tradeoff is higher radial (separating) force on the shafts and bearings for the same transmitted torque, along with slightly more sensitivity to small center-distance errors. 20° has become the default because it's a reasonable middle ground for most general-purpose power transmission; 14.5° persists mostly in older equipment and some specialty low-noise applications where the shallower angle's smoother engagement is worth the weaker tooth root.

The one rule that can't be bent: **both gears in a mesh must share the same module and the same pressure angle.** A mismatch on either one means the tooth profiles simply don't have compatible shapes, no matter how close the tooth counts or center distance look on paper.

## Why gear teeth use an involute curve at all

The curved flank of a gear tooth isn't an arbitrary rounded shape — it's a specific mathematical curve called an involute, generated by unwinding a taut string from a circle (the base circle) and tracing the path its end draws. The property that makes this curve valuable for gearing, rather than just elegant, is that two involute profiles in mesh maintain a constant velocity ratio throughout the entire engagement, even if the center distance between the gears isn't held to a perfectly exact value. That tolerance to small center-distance error is a big part of why the involute became the near-universal standard: real assemblies have real tolerance stacks, and a tooth form that keeps working correctly despite them is worth far more in practice than a theoretically "purer" curve that doesn't forgive any error at all.

## Tooth count: the ratio decision, with a floor

Tooth count is usually driven by the speed or torque ratio you need between two shafts — a small driver gear and a large driven gear multiplies torque and reduces speed, and vice versa. But tooth count has a practical lower bound that isn't obvious until you hit it: cut too few teeth at a given pressure angle, and the cutting process undercuts the tooth root, removing material right where the tooth needs it most for strength and shortening the usable involute profile. At 20° pressure angle, that floor is generally around 17 teeth for standard full-depth gears (lower with profile-shifted designs, which push the cutter outward to compensate — a more advanced technique worth knowing exists, but outside the scope of a basic design pass).

At the other end, more teeth (for a given pitch diameter) means smaller, more numerous teeth, individually weaker but engaging more smoothly with a higher contact ratio — more tooth pairs sharing the load at any instant, which tends to run quieter. Gear design is usually a balance between these two pulls rather than a single "correct" answer: enough teeth to run smoothly and avoid undercut, few enough that each tooth stays big enough to carry the load.

## Helical gears: the same basics, twisted

Everything above applies identically to helical gears — module, pressure angle, and the involute tooth form are all defined the same way. The difference is that the tooth profile is swept along the gear's axis with a twist, defined by the helix angle, rather than extruded straight. That twist means teeth engage gradually along their length instead of all at once across the full face width, which is why helical gears typically run quieter and can carry more load for the same size — at the cost of introducing an axial thrust load that the bearings and housing need to be designed to handle, which spur gears don't produce.

## Backlash: the deliberate gap

A meshing pair of gears is never cut to a mathematically exact zero-clearance fit — some backlash, a small amount of free play between mating tooth flanks, is deliberately designed in. Without it, ordinary thermal expansion, manufacturing tolerance, and deflection under load would cause teeth to bind rather than mesh smoothly, particularly as the gears heat up during operation and expand slightly. Backlash is typically introduced by cutting the tooth slightly thinner than the theoretical involute profile calls for, or by opening up the center distance a controlled amount, rather than by changing module or pressure angle.

The tradeoff is direct: more backlash means smoother, more forgiving operation and more tolerance for thermal growth and manufacturing variation, but it also means more positional error every time the drive direction reverses — a real problem for any application where precise position control matters, like a CNC axis or a servo-driven mechanism. Precision motion applications often specify significantly tighter backlash than general power-transmission gearing would use, sometimes at the cost of needing tighter manufacturing tolerances or an anti-backlash design (a spring-loaded split gear, for instance) to hold that precision without binding.

## A sensible design order

In practice, the decisions tend to fall in this order: pick the tooth count ratio your application needs first, since that's usually fixed by the speed or torque requirement. Then pick a module based on available space and the load the teeth need to carry — a quick way to sanity-check the load side is running the transmitted torque and speed through a [shaft torque calculator](/calculators/shaft-torque/) to confirm what torque the teeth actually need to survive. Confirm the resulting pitch diameter fits your center-distance constraint. Then generate the actual tooth geometry.
